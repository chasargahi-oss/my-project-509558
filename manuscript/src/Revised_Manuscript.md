# Species identity outweighs native–introduced provenance in controlling soil organic carbon beneath amenity trees in a hyper-arid industrial plantation

**Running head:** Species identity and soil carbon in a hyper-arid plantation

## Abstract

**Purpose** Industrial plantations on hyper-arid coasts are expanding, yet their soil organic carbon (SOC) is largely unquantified and species selection still rests on the native–introduced distinction, a label rather than a functional trait. We asked how much SOC variation beneath such planting is attributable to species identity and how much to provenance.

**Methods** In the South Pars zone (Assaluyeh, Iran; 157 mm mean annual rainfall, 29.9 °C), we sampled soil beneath three trees of each of three native (*Phoenix dactylifera*, *Ziziphus spina-christi*, *Prosopis cineraria*) and three introduced species (*Eucalyptus camaldulensis*, *Callistemon viminalis*, *Conocarpus erectus*) at 0–10 and 10–30 cm (36 samples). SOC was determined by Walkley–Black oxidation and texture by the hydrometer method, and the variance was partitioned hierarchically.

**Results** Species identity accounted for 0.46 of multivariate dispersion and provenance for 0.12, but the terms differ fivefold in degrees of freedom; partitioned, differences among species within a provenance (0.34) exceeded differences between provenances. SOC ranged from 0.59% under *C. erectus* to 2.46% under *P. dactylifera*, the richest stand being among the sandiest, and declined by 32% with depth without a species × depth interaction. Native stands held 1.60% against 0.93% (*g* = 0.90, *p* = 0.010) but occupied siltier soils, so the contrast is confounded with planting position. Stocks were 20.6–98.4 Mg C ha^−1^ at an assumed bulk density of 1.4 g cm^−3^.

**Conclusions** Under input limitation, species identity governs SOC and provenance adds little; selection should be species-level. This first inventory for a hyper-arid Gulf plantation extends identity dominance beyond temperate forests.

**Keywords** carbon sequestration · dryland afforestation · species selection · carbon stratification · soil texture · Persian Gulf

## Introduction

Soils hold more organic carbon than the atmosphere and vegetation combined, and the size of that pool is sensitive to land use and to the identity of the plants that occupy a site (Lal 2004, 2005). Drylands, which cover roughly 40% of the terrestrial surface, are the setting in which this sensitivity is least constrained by water and most constrained by organic-matter supply (Farage et al. 2007; Woomer et al. 2004). Along the hyper-arid coasts of the Persian Gulf, industrial development has produced a distinct and fast-growing land-use class: irrigated amenity and shelterbelt plantations established on saline, calcareous, sand-dominated soils to suppress dust, screen process units and green the perimeter of petrochemical complexes. The South Pars Special Economic Energy Zone at Assaluyeh, the largest industrial development in Iran, extends over approximately 45 km² of such ground and is planted almost entirely with a handful of trees chosen for establishment success rather than for any belowground function. Whether these plantings accumulate soil carbon, and which species do so most effectively, has not previously been measured.

Species-selection guidance in restoration and plantation forestry is still dominated by the native-versus-introduced dichotomy, which carries an implicit expectation that native species support larger soil carbon pools. Evidence for that expectation is real but compartment-specific. In mangroves, native species can exceed introduced congeners in sediment carbon accumulation (He et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2022; Barona-Obando et al. 2026), and a global meta-analysis of 1,678 paired comparisons found that non-native forests stored more total carbon, through greater aboveground biomass, while native forests stored more belowground carbon, particularly soil organic carbon (Lázaro-Lobo et al. 2025). In southern Iran, topsoil organic matter and nutrients were higher beneath native *Prosopis cineraria* and *Acacia* spp. than beneath invasive *Prosopis juliflora* (Moslehi Jouybari et al. 2022), and two congeneric *Prosopis* species differed markedly in their effects on soil fertility and salinity (Moslehi et al. 2025). Two features of this literature matter here. First, provenance effects are moderated by climate and by compartment, so a global average is a weak basis for local decisions (Lázaro-Lobo et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022). Second, provenance is a historical label rather than a functional trait: litter decomposability, rooting depth, mycorrhizal association and tissue stoichiometry vary at least as much within a provenance as between provenances, and it is those traits that govern belowground processes (Angst et al. 2019; Peng et al. 2020; Hüblová et al. 2026). Where congeners diverge, the label cannot carry the mechanism.

A parallel and largely separate literature on temperate and boreal forests has asked the same question in a different form: how much soil-carbon variation is attributable to tree species identity at all? Common-garden and paired-plot studies repeatedly find substantial identity effects on forest-floor and mineral soil carbon (Vesterdal et al. 2013; Andivia et al. 2016; Díaz-Pinés et al. 2011; Diers et al. 2021; Steffens et al. 2022a, b), and where identity has been tested against diversity, identity usually explains more of the variation in stocks, C/N ratio and pH (Dawud et al. 2016; Osei et al. 2021; Ureña-Lara et al. 2026; Tang et al. 2024). The mediating traits are increasingly resolved: litter quality and quantity set the input, root traits determine its placement, and microbial processing and mineral association determine retention (Angst et al. 2019; Beugnon et al. 2023; Lorenz and Thiele-Bruhn 2019; Zheng et al. 2022; Staszel-Szlachta et al. 2025). What remains unclear is the boundary conditions. These studies are overwhelmingly located in mesic temperate and boreal forests on developed soils, where both supply and stabilisation capacity are relatively high. Theory predicts that identity effects should strengthen, not weaken, where aridity constrains decomposition and where organic input rather than mineral surface area limits the pool (Yan et al. 2026; Augusto and Boča 2022), but direct tests in hyper-arid, calcareous, saline coastal systems are missing. The few dryland studies available concern rangelands, shelterbelts or semiarid afforestation, and they do not separate species identity from provenance, nor do they account for the fact that native and introduced stands are usually planted on different ground.

This study addresses that gap with the first soil-carbon inventory of a hyper-arid industrial plantation on the northern Persian Gulf coast, and with an analytical design that makes the comparison between identity and provenance explicit rather than incidental. Three elements constitute the advance over the current state of the art. First, we quantify SOC and texture beneath six species — three native and three introduced — that are planted at industrial scale across the Gulf, including *Conocarpus erectus*, which has been deployed in its millions across the region over the past two decades and has never been evaluated for soil carbon. Second, we partition the variance hierarchically, so that the identity term and the provenance term are compared on a like-for-like basis and not simply by the magnitude of an omnibus R² that depends on degrees of freedom. Third, we test whether the provenance signal is separable from the textural differences that accompany planting history, and we report the resulting uncertainty explicitly rather than treating provenance as a causal agent. Together these elements allow a statement about mechanism — input limitation rather than mineral protection governs the pool — that is transferable beyond the site.

We tested three hypotheses. **H1:** variation in SOC among species within a provenance exceeds variation between provenances, so that species identity explains more of the total variation than provenance. **H2:** stands of native species hold more SOC than stands of introduced species. **H3:** SOC declines with depth and the decline is common to all species, that is, there is no species × depth interaction. The corresponding objectives were to quantify SOC, organic matter and particle-size distribution beneath the six species; to partition the variance in SOC and in the multivariate soil properties between identity and provenance; to test the provenance contrast and to establish how much of it can be attributed to differences in soil texture and planting position; and to translate the concentrations into areal stocks with an explicit treatment of bulk-density uncertainty.

## Materials and methods

### Site description

The study was carried out in the South Pars Special Economic Energy Zone at Assaluyeh, Bushehr Province, southern Iran, on the northern shore of the Persian Gulf (approximately 27°29′–27°32′N, 52°33′–52°40′E; 300 km east of Bushehr and 570 km west of Bandar Abbas). The zone occupies about 45 km² of the Assaluyeh alluvial plain, a catchment of roughly 285 km² that is bounded by the Assaluyeh and Gaubandi anticlines to the north-east and east, the Khalfani anticline to the south-west, and the Persian Gulf to the south. Mean annual rainfall is 157 mm, concentrated in winter, and mean annual temperature is 29.9 °C with summer maxima above 45 °C, giving a hyper-arid to arid coastal climate in which potential evaporation greatly exceeds precipitation. Soils are calcareous and saline, developed on Quaternary alluvium and, in the surrounding exposures, on the Bakhtiari and Aghajari formations; they are coarse textured, weakly structured and low in organic matter, and they are irrigated throughout the year with brackish groundwater to sustain the plantings. Vegetation consists of irrigated amenity and shelterbelt stands of native and introduced trees maintained by the zone authority; there is no native forest in the immediate area and no unplanted reference soil of comparable irrigation history.

### Experimental design and treatments

The study is an observational survey of established amenity stands, not a manipulative experiment, and we describe it in those terms because the distinction governs the strength of the inferences drawn below. The two factors were tree species (six levels) and soil depth (two levels: 0–10 and 10–30 cm). Species were grouped a posteriori into two provenances: native (*Phoenix dactylifera* L., *Ziziphus spina-christi* (L.) Desf., *Prosopis cineraria* (L.) Druce) and introduced (*Eucalyptus camaldulensis* Dehnh., *Callistemon viminalis* (Sol. ex Gaertn.) G. Don, *Conocarpus erectus* L.). The provenance factor therefore has three species nested within each level, and species, not provenance, is the level at which independent stands exist. Three trees per species were selected at random from the stands of that species within the zone, giving 18 trees; each tree was sampled at both depths, giving 36 samples. The individual tree is the unit of replication for species comparisons, and the two depths are repeated measures within a tree. Because species are not randomly assigned to ground, the provenance contrast is partly a contrast between planting positions: native stands occupy older, finer-textured ground close to the original settlement, whereas the introduced ornamentals, including *C. erectus*, were planted more recently on graded and disturbed roadside and perimeter strips. This confounding is quantified and discussed rather than assumed away.

### Soil sampling

Sampling took place in late winter (February–March 2017), before the spring flush of growth and litterfall, so that all stands were sampled in the same phenological window. At each selected tree, soil was collected with a hand auger from two increments, 0–10 cm and 10–30 cm, at a constant distance from the trunk and at a consistent position relative to the canopy. Each sample was a composite of several cores taken around that position, so that the sample represents the soil of the immediate rooting zone rather than a single point. Samples were stored in clear plastic bags, air-dried in the shade, freed of visible plant residues and coarse fragments, disaggregated and passed through a 2 mm sieve before analysis. Sampling depth was limited to 30 cm by the auger and by the presence of gravelly horizons at some positions; the consequences of this truncation are considered in the Discussion.

### Laboratory analyses

Organic carbon was determined by wet dichromate oxidation following Walkley and Black (1934). No correction factor was applied for incomplete oxidation, so the concentrations reported here are conservative and are likely to underestimate true SOC by roughly one-fifth relative to a corrected determination; this does not affect comparisons among species, because all samples were treated identically, but it does mean that the stocks below should be read as lower bounds. Organic matter was obtained from organic carbon with the Van Bemmelen factor of 1.724. Because organic matter is a deterministic multiple of organic carbon, all inferential analyses used organic carbon alone, and organic matter is reported only for comparability with earlier studies.

Particle-size distribution was measured by the hydrometer method (Bouyoucos 1962) after dispersion in sodium hexametaphosphate, and textural classes were assigned with the USDA textural triangle (Soil Survey Division Staff 1993). Fractions were closed to 100% before analysis. Bulk density was not measured in this survey. This is the principal limitation of the stock calculation and is treated explicitly in the following section and in the Discussion.

### Bulk density and calculation of areal stocks

Areal stocks were calculated as the product of organic carbon concentration, bulk density and increment thickness. Because bulk density was not determined, a constant value of 1.4 g cm^−3^ was adopted, which is representative of coarse-textured mineral soils of this region and lies within the range reported for irrigated sandy loam soils under plantation. Stocks were then recalculated at 1.2 and 1.6 g cm^−3^ to bracket the plausible range and to show how strongly the areal quantities depend on the assumption. Two consequences follow and are stated here rather than relegated to the Discussion. First, holding bulk density constant suppresses any real differences in compaction or porosity among stands, so the stocks cannot be read as evidence about soil physical condition. Second, organic matter lowers bulk density, so a constant value probably overstates the stocks of the carbon-rich stands and therefore compresses the contrasts between species; the true contrast in stocks is likely to be wider than the one we report, not narrower. Stocks are presented as indicative quantities that place concentrations on a management-relevant scale, and the equivalent-soil-mass basis, which is now preferred for change detection, is recommended for future monitoring rather than applied retrospectively to a single sampling date.

### Statistical analysis

All analyses were carried out on the 36 samples, with tree-level means used as a conservative check against pseudoreplication (Hurlbert 1984). Organic carbon was right-skewed, with variance increasing in the mean, and was therefore ln-transformed for parametric modelling; transformation restored normality of residuals (Shapiro–Wilk *W* = 0.979, *p* = 0.727) and homogeneity of variance across species (Levene *p* = 0.406). Untransformed values are reported descriptively.

Species and depth effects were assessed by two-way factorial analysis of variance with Type II sums of squares, reporting partial eta squared and omega squared as effect sizes; omega squared is the less biased estimator at this sample size and is the value we interpret. Pairwise species contrasts used Tukey's honestly significant difference procedure and are summarised as a compact letter display derived from maximal cliques of the non-significance graph (Piepho 2004). Provenance contrasts used Welch's *t* test with the Mann–Whitney *U* test as a distribution-free check, Hedges' *g* as effect size and bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples). The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure controlled the false discovery rate across the four soil properties (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Depth was tested both as a fixed effect in the factorial model and as a paired contrast within trees.

Multivariate structure was examined by principal component analysis of z-standardised variables, and group differences were tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance on Euclidean distances with 9,999 permutations (Anderson 2001). Because the species factor and the provenance factor differ in degrees of freedom, the omnibus R² values are not directly comparable, and we therefore also report a hierarchical partition in which the species sum of squares is decomposed into the provenance component (1 df) and the component for species within provenance (4 df). To respect the nesting of depths within trees, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted to ln-transformed organic carbon with provenance and depth as fixed effects and tree as a random intercept, and the intraclass correlation was used to quantify the dependence introduced by sampling two increments per tree. Retrospective power was calculated for the observed effect sizes. Analyses used Python 3.13 (pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn) with fixed random seeds, and all scripts are provided with the data.

### Biological material and legislative compliance

No wild plant populations or accessions were collected. All trees were established amenity plantings identified from planting records and morphology, so no herbarium voucher deposition was required. No bacteria, yeasts, mycorrhizal strains or new gene sequences were used or generated, so no culture-collection or NCBI accession numbers apply. No genetic resources were obtained from local collectors or protected areas, and no access and benefit-sharing obligations under the Nagoya Protocol were triggered. The study involved no genetically engineered plants, quarantine organisms or new taxa requiring registration, and no permissions under phytosanitary or biosafety legislation were required.

## Results

### Soil properties and the species ranking

Organic carbon spanned more than an order of magnitude across the 36 samples (0.24–3.72%, mean 1.26%, CV 63%), whereas the particle-size fractions were comparatively conservative (CV 25–27%), a pattern consistent with a common parent material and a carbon signal that is not simply derived from texture (Table 1). All samples fell in the sandy loam or loam classes, and the species means spanned only 10.2–14.3% clay, so the six stands were compared on broadly similar substrates (Table 1; Fig. 3). The ranking of species means was, however, strongly structured, and it cannot be attributed to texture: the species with the highest concentration was also the second sandiest (62.4% sand), while the species with the least carbon held an intermediate sand content. *P. dactylifera* was separated from the rest at 2.46%, followed by *Z. spina-christi*, *E. camaldulensis*, *P. cineraria*, *C. viminalis* and *C. erectus* (Fig. 1a). The ranking did not sort by provenance, because the introduced *E. camaldulensis* ranked third of six and above two native species. Dispersion differed markedly among species, with the coefficient of variation ranging from 32% under *Z. spina-christi* to 69% under *C. erectus*, so species means are not equally informative and the individual-level distributions are shown alongside them.

### Species identity

Species identity was a strong and consistent source of variation in organic carbon. In the factorial model, species accounted for a substantial share of the variance in ln-transformed carbon (F~5,24~ = 8.27, *p* < 0.001), and the effect size was large and robust to small-sample bias (partial η² = 0.63, ω² = 0.46); the model as a whole explained 70% of the variance (Table 2). Species also differed in sand and silt (*p* < 0.001 in both cases) but not in clay (*p* = 0.161), which indicates that the stands differ in the sand–silt balance rather than in clay content.

Pairwise contrasts separated the extremes and left the middle of the ranking unresolved (Table 3; Fig. 1a). *P. dactylifera* exceeded *C. erectus*, *Z. spina-christi* exceeded *C. erectus*, and the date palm also exceeded *C. viminalis* and *P. cineraria*. The four middle-ranked species could not be separated from one another at the 5% level. *E. camaldulensis* was statistically indistinguishable from every species despite an intermediate mean, and the reason is dispersion rather than position: its individual values spanned 0.43–2.43%, the widest range of any species, which widened its confidence interval to overlap the entire ranking. A species that cannot be characterised by its mean is itself a result, and we return to it below.

### Provenance and its confounding with texture

Stands of native species held 1.60 ± 0.87% organic carbon against 0.93 ± 0.55% in stands of introduced species, a difference of 0.67 percentage points (95% bootstrap interval 0.21–1.14; *t* = 2.76, *p* = 0.010; Hedges' *g* = 0.90; Mann–Whitney *U* = 242, *p* = 0.012) (Table 5; Fig. 1b). This supports H2 in direction and magnitude. The same grouping, however, also differed in texture: native stands held less sand (51.9% versus 58.8%, *p* = 0.032) and more silt (35.0% versus 28.6%, *p* = 0.020), while clay content did not differ (*p* = 0.705). After false-discovery-rate adjustment across the four properties, carbon, sand and silt remained significant and clay remained non-significant. The provenance contrast is therefore not a clean contrast between two groups of trees on the same substrate; it is a contrast between two groups of trees that also occupy soils differing systematically in the sand–silt balance, which is precisely the fraction that retains organic matter most effectively in coarse calcareous soils.

### Vertical distribution

Organic carbon declined from 1.51% at 0–10 cm to 1.02% at 10–30 cm, a difference of 0.49 percentage points within trees (*t*~17~ = 3.70, *p* = 0.002, *d*~z~ = 0.87), equivalent to a 32% reduction (Table 4; Fig. 2a). Clay moved in the opposite direction, rising from 11.7% to 14.0% (*p* < 0.001, *d*~z~ = −1.09), whereas sand and silt showed no vertical change. Surface enrichment in carbon together with subsurface enrichment in clay is the pattern expected where organic inputs enter from above and where clay has translocated downward, and it has a direct consequence for the interpretation of texture–carbon relationships, discussed below.

The factorial model detected a depth effect of moderate size (*p* = 0.006, partial η² = 0.28, ω² = 0.10) and no species × depth interaction (F~5,24~ = 0.83, *p* = 0.540, ω² < 0), which supports H3: the ranking of species at the surface persists at depth, and the main effects can be read directly from the marginal means. The vertical gradient is therefore a property of the system rather than of particular species. Stratification ratios, the quotient of surface and subsurface concentrations, averaged 1.48 but varied widely among species, from 1.29 under *Z. spina-christi* to 3.15 under *C. erectus*. Read in isolation, ratios above 2 indicate active accrual at the surface (Franzluebbers 2002), which would rank *C. erectus* as the most improved stand. That reading does not survive inspection: its high ratio arises from an exceptionally carbon-poor subsoil (0.29%) as the denominator, not from vigorous surface accumulation, and the species holds the least carbon at both depths. *P. dactylifera* shows the converse pattern, a modest ratio of 1.35 combined with the highest concentrations in both increments. Ratio and concentration carry complementary information, and neither substitutes for the other.

### Multivariate structure and variance partitioning

Two principal components exceeded unit eigenvalue and together captured 89% of the variance in the standardised soil properties (Table 6; Fig. 5). The first component (57%) is a texture axis, with sand loading positively and silt and clay negatively, and the second (33%) is dominated by organic carbon. The near-orthogonality of the carbon and texture axes indicates that the carbon signal is not a passive reflection of particle-size distribution, which is the multivariate counterpart of the observation that clay did not differ among species.

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance attributed 0.46 of the dispersion to species identity (pseudo-F = 5.05, *p* < 0.001) and 0.12 to provenance (pseudo-F = 4.49, *p* = 0.010). Because these two terms have five and one degrees of freedom respectively, the raw comparison overstates the difference between them: provenance is the more efficient predictor per degree of freedom, and the species term includes the provenance term. Partitioning the species sum of squares hierarchically resolves the ambiguity (Table 7). Differences between provenances account for 0.12 of the total dispersion, differences among species within a provenance for a further 0.34, and the residual for 0.54. The correct statement of H1 is therefore that variation among species within a provenance is roughly three times the variation between provenances, not that provenance is unimportant per se. Collapsing six species into two categories discards a third of the explainable structure.

The linear mixed-effects model, which accounts for the two depths being measured on the same tree, retained both fixed effects: introduced provenance lowered ln-carbon by 0.58 log units (z = −2.60, *p* = 0.009, a 44% reduction in concentration) and the deeper increment by 0.44 log units (z = −3.17, *p* = 0.002). The intraclass correlation was 0.44, confirming that treating the 36 samples as independent would have understated the standard errors of the provenance contrast by a meaningful margin. At the sample level, carbon correlated negatively with clay (*r* = −0.51, *p* = 0.001) and not with sand or silt; the strong sand–silt coupling (*r* = −0.95) is a closure artefact of compositional data and is not interpreted.

### Areal stocks and their uncertainty

At the reference bulk density of 1.4 g cm^−3^, 0–30 cm stocks ranged from 20.6 Mg C ha^−1^ under *C. erectus* to 98.4 Mg C ha^−1^ under *P. dactylifera*, and stands of native species averaged 63.4 ± 33.5 against 35.8 ± 14.1 Mg C ha^−1^ for introduced species (ratio 1.77, *p* = 0.044) (Table 8; Fig. 4). Although carbon is more concentrated near the surface, the 10–30 cm increment holds the larger stock in five of the six species and in 14 of the 18 trees, because it is twice as thick; the all-species mean stock is 21.1 Mg C ha^−1^ in the upper and 28.5 Mg C ha^−1^ in the lower increment. Depth-weighted accounting therefore places more of the resource below 10 cm than concentrations alone suggest, which matters because deeper carbon is generally more persistent. *C. erectus* is the sole exception, its subsoil so depleted that the thicker layer holds less carbon than the thinner one.

Because bulk density was assumed rather than measured, the stocks were recalculated across the plausible range of 1.2–1.6 g cm^−3^. All stocks scale linearly with the assumption and the species ranking is invariant, but the absolute range widens to 17.6–112.5 Mg C ha^−1^ (Table 8). The uncertainty in the areal quantities is therefore dominated by a single unmeasured parameter, and this is the main reason we treat the stocks as indicative and the concentrations as the primary result.

### Robustness and sensitivity

Repeating the principal tests on tree-level means (*n* = 18) preserved the species effect (F~5,12~ = 6.08, *p* = 0.005) and the provenance effect, the latter becoming marginal (*t* = 2.22, *p* = 0.049). No outliers were detected in the factorial model (maximum studentised residual 3.11 against a Bonferroni critical value of 3.63). Retrospective power exceeded 0.99 for the species effect but was 0.76 for provenance, and reaching 0.80 at the observed effect size would require approximately 20 samples per group. The provenance contrast is thus adequately sized to be detected but not adequately replicated to be estimated precisely, a distinction that governs how strongly it can be used in management recommendations.

## Discussion

The central finding of this study is that the identity of the planted species, not its provenance, is the operative control on soil organic carbon in this hyper-arid industrial plantation. Six species planted on the same coastal plain, within the same 45 km² zone and under the same irrigation regime, produced a 4.2-fold range in SOC, and the ranking cut across the native–introduced division: an introduced eucalypt ranked above two native species. When the variance was partitioned hierarchically, differences among species within a provenance were roughly three times the differences between provenances. This is the first quantification of that partition for a hyper-arid, calcareous, saline coastal plantation, and it matters because species-selection advice for such settings is currently organised around the provenance label, which our data show to be a weak proxy for the trait that actually governs the pool.

The reason provenance fails as a predictor is that it describes history rather than function. Litter decomposability, rooting depth and distribution, mycorrhizal association and tissue stoichiometry determine how much organic matter enters the soil, where it enters, and how quickly it is processed (Angst et al. 2019; Beugnon et al. 2023; Hüblová et al. 2026; Peng et al. 2020), and none of these traits is fixed by geographic origin. Our results reproduce, at the scale of a single industrial zone, what congeneric comparisons have shown at the scale of a genus: two *Prosopis* species of the same provenance differed markedly in their effects on soil fertility and salinity in southern Iran (Moslehi et al. 2025), and in the present data the lowest-carbon species is an introduced ornamental while the third-ranked is an introduced eucalypt. The practical implication is that provenance-based rules of thumb, including the reasonable-sounding preference for natives, will misassign species whenever the trait differences that matter do not align with origin — which, in a flora assembled for ornamental and establishment criteria, they largely do not.

The performance of *P. dactylifera* provides the clearest mechanistic lead, and it is one that the trait framework anticipates. As a monocot with a fibrous, adventitious root system and no secondary thickening, the date palm falls outside the broadleaf–conifer contrast that organises most of the temperate literature, and its high SOC cannot be attributed to biomass, which is modest. Three features of its management plausibly combine: coarse frond litter that accumulates at the surface rather than dispersing, the practice of stacking pruned fronds and bunch residues at the base of the trunk, and a dense mat of fine roots in the upper profile that turns over in place. Together these would deliver a continuous, locally concentrated input at both sampled depths, which is exactly the pattern observed — the highest concentrations in both increments combined with one of the lowest stratification ratios. We did not measure litter mass, litter chemistry or root density, so this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism, but it is a testable one, and it is the mechanism most likely to be transferable to other hot coastal plantations where the date palm is already the dominant native tree.

The opposite extreme, *C. erectus*, carries the most direct management consequence because of the scale at which it has been deployed. It held the lowest concentrations at both depths and the most impoverished subsoil in the study, a result that is difficult to attribute to youth alone given that co-planted introduced species on comparable ground reached three times its concentration. Its stratification ratio of 3.15 illustrates a second, methodological point. Ratios above 2 are conventionally read as evidence of active surface accrual (Franzluebbers 2002), and on that criterion *C. erectus* would be judged the best-performing stand in the study. The ratio is inflated by a small denominator rather than a large numerator, a limitation implicit in the original formulation but easily overlooked when the index is used alone. Indices of distribution must therefore be reported with the concentrations they derive from, and where the two disagree the concentration is the more defensible quantity.

The negative correlation between carbon and clay, which appears to contradict the expectation of organo-mineral stabilisation, resolves once the vertical structure is taken into account. Clay increases with depth while carbon decreases, so a pooled correlation across increments is partly an artefact of that contrast. Beyond this, clay spanned only 3–20% and did not differ among species, which places these soils well below the range in which mineral surface area typically limits retention, and the near-orthogonal carbon and texture axes of the principal component analysis confirm that the carbon signal is carried independently of particle size. The species with the highest concentration in the study was also one of the two sandiest, which is difficult to reconcile with any textural control of the ranking. The interpretation we favour is that the system is limited by the supply of organic matter rather than by its protection: where inputs are scarce, their variation dominates the pool, and mineral capacity is never approached. This is consistent with evidence that stabilisation requires both reactive surfaces and sufficient substrate (Staszel-Szlachta et al. 2025; Su et al. 2021), that microbial processing into mineral-associated forms is substrate-constrained (Beugnon et al. 2023; Zheng et al. 2022), and that trait effects interact with site properties rather than acting uniformly (Augusto and Boča 2022). It also explains why identity emerges as the leading factor in this system but not necessarily in temperate forests, where retention capacity is more often the binding constraint.

The provenance contrast requires a more cautious reading than its statistics suggest, and we state the limitation plainly. Native stands held 1.7 times more carbon than introduced stands, the direction expected from the global belowground pattern (Lázaro-Lobo et al. 2025) and from Iranian comparisons of native and invasive *Prosopis* (Moslehi Jouybari et al. 2022), and the effect survived false-discovery-rate adjustment and tree-level re-analysis. But native stands also occupied siltier, less sandy ground, and in these coarse calcareous soils the silt fraction is the more retentive one; the two groups additionally differ in planting history, with natives on older ground and ornamentals on recently graded roadside strips. Without random assignment of species to positions, tree effects and site effects cannot be separated, and the observed difference is an upper bound on what the trees themselves contribute. The honest conclusion is that the direction is reliable, the magnitude is not, and the contrast should be treated as hypothesis-generating. Resolving it requires a matched-pair design in which species are compared on the same soil unit, or a common garden with assigned positions.

Two further features of the results bear on how the system should be interpreted. First, the absence of a species × depth interaction, together with a 32% decline in concentration between increments, indicates that the vertical gradient is a property of the plantation environment — inputs entering from above, clay moving downward — while species modulate the level of the whole profile rather than its shape. This distinction matters for monitoring, because it means that surface sampling captures the species signal efficiently, but it also means that the larger part of the stock, which lies below 10 cm, is invisible to surface-only surveys. Second, the heterogeneity within *E. camaldulensis* (CV 60%) is not a nuisance to be averaged away but a signal that stand-level covariates we did not record — age, spacing, irrigation history, canopy condition — matter at least as much as species for this taxon. Its mean is uninformative precisely because its stands are not equivalent, and future work should sample stand covariates rather than more trees.

The novelty of the study lies less in confirming that trees differ than in establishing where the difference resides and what that implies for policy. Industrial and amenity plantations on the Gulf coast are expanding, are irrigated with brackish water, and are planted overwhelmingly with a small set of ornamental species; the *Conocarpus* programme alone represents millions of individuals across the region. Our data indicate that substituting species within a planting programme is a lever with a fourfold effect on SOC concentration, that this lever is not aligned with the native–introduced distinction on which current guidance rests, and that the resulting areal differences are large enough to matter for carbon accounting even under the most conservative bulk-density assumption. They also indicate the direction in which that accounting must be improved: with a single assumed bulk density, the uncertainty in the stock estimate exceeds the differences among species, which is an argument for measurement rather than for more sampling.

The limitations of the study define the boundary of these claims and are worth stating without hedging. Replication is modest — three trees per species, one zone, one sampling occasion — so the species effect, though large and well powered, rests on a narrow sample of stand conditions, and the provenance effect, at power 0.76, is suggestive rather than settled. Bulk density was not measured, so the stocks inherit a fixed assumption and ignore coarse fragments and the equivalent-soil-mass basis; because organic matter lowers bulk density, the constant value probably compresses rather than inflates the contrast among species. Sampling stopped at 30 cm, which understates the deep-rooted phreatophytes and *P. cineraria* most of all, and it cannot capture the subsoil pool in which carbon is most persistent. Stand covariates were not recorded, so age and irrigation history remain uncontrolled. We measured soil carbon only: no nitrogen, phosphorus, pH, electrical conductivity or calcium carbonate, and no litter or root traits, so the mechanistic interpretation advanced above is inferential. Finally, the study addresses one compartment, and compartment is consequential: globally, non-native forests hold more aboveground carbon while native forests hold more belowground (Lázaro-Lobo et al. 2025), and a fast-growing eucalypt with substantial biomass may rank differently in a whole-ecosystem budget than it does in the soil alone.

## Conclusions

Soil organic carbon beneath amenity trees at Assaluyeh is controlled principally by species identity. The first hypothesis was supported in its substantive form: differences among species within a provenance were about three times the differences between provenances, and the six species spanned a 4.2-fold range in concentration. The second hypothesis was supported in direction, with native stands holding 1.7 times more carbon than introduced stands, but the provenance contrast is confounded with soil texture and planting history and should not be read as a causal effect of origin. The third hypothesis was supported: carbon declined by 32% between increments while clay increased, and the absence of a species × depth interaction shows that species shift the level of the profile rather than its shape.

The mechanistic reading is that this system is limited by the supply of organic matter rather than by the capacity of the soil to protect it. Clay contents were low, did not differ among species, and were uncorrelated with carbon once the vertical structure is taken into account; carbon and texture loaded on near-orthogonal axes. Under such input limitation, the traits that govern litter quality and root distribution — not geographic origin — determine how much carbon enters the soil and where it is placed, which is why identity dominates and provenance does not.

For plantation design the implication is direct: select species on measured performance, not on provenance category. Within the species tested, *P. dactylifera* and *Z. spina-christi* are the strongest candidates on carbon grounds and are also tolerant of drought and salinity, whereas the case for continued large-scale planting of *C. erectus* on carbon grounds is weak, although its value for dust suppression and rapid establishment must be weighed separately. Because most of the 0–30 cm stock lies below 10 cm, subsoil protection deserves as much attention as surface management. Closing the remaining uncertainty requires measured bulk density and equivalent-soil-mass accounting, profiles deeper than 30 cm, stand covariates, and ultimately a common-garden design in which species are compared on assigned ground; until such data exist, species-level performance remains the most defensible basis for decisions and the provenance label the least.
